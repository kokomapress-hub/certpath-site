4 ACT Math questions, worked step by step

These four come from CertPath's free 25-question ACT Math sample set and are quoted exactly as written there.

Question 1 — Algebra: a caterer's fixed fee and per-guest charge

A caterer charges a fixed setup fee plus a constant charge per guest. An event for 40 guests is billed $1,010, and an event for 65 guests is billed $1,510. At the same rates, what is the bill for an event for 100 guests?

  • A. $2,000
  • B. $2,323
  • C. $2,210
  • D. $2,525

Answer: C. The extra 25 guests add $500, so the per-guest charge is $20. The setup fee is 1,010 − 40(20) = $210. For 100 guests the bill is 210 + 100(20) = $2,210.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you can split a linear relationship into its fixed and variable parts before scaling it up. Choice D, $2,525, comes from scaling the $1,010 bill proportionally to 100 guests, which wrongly assumes the total is proportional to the number of guests even though a fixed fee is baked in.

Question 2 — Geometry: how far apart are two hikers?

Two straight trails leave a ranger station, forming a 68° angle with each other. One hiker walks 12 km along one trail, and a second hiker walks 17 km along the other. To the nearest tenth of a kilometre, how far apart are the two hikers? (cos 68° ≈ 0.3746, sin 68° ≈ 0.9272)

  • A. 20.8 km
  • B. 24.2 km
  • C. 7.4 km
  • D. 16.7 km

Answer: D. By the law of cosines, the distance squared is 12² + 17² − 2(12)(17)cos 68° = 433 − 408(0.3746) ≈ 280.2, so the distance is about 16.7 km.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you reach for the law of cosines once a triangle isn't a right triangle. Choice A, 20.8 km, is √433 — treating the 68° angle as if it were 90° and applying the plain Pythagorean theorem instead. Adding rather than subtracting the cosine term gives the other wrong answer, about 24.2 km.

Question 3 — Statistics & Probability: reversing a conditional

At a clinic, 40% of patients take a certain medication. Among patients who take it, 12% report headaches; among patients who do not take it, 3% report headaches. One patient is selected at random from those who report headaches. To the nearest hundredth, what is the probability that this patient takes the medication?

  • A. 0.73
  • B. 0.12
  • C. 0.27
  • D. 0.40

Answer: A. Of every 100 patients, 40(0.12) = 4.8 take the medication and report headaches, while 60(0.03) = 1.8 do not take it and report headaches. Among the 6.6 headache reporters, the share taking the medication is 4.8/6.6 ≈ 0.73.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you can reverse a conditional probability instead of reporting the one given in the setup. Choice B, 0.12, is the probability of a headache given the medication — the opposite direction from what the question actually asks.

Question 4 — Integrating Essential Skills: markup, then two discounts

A store marks up each jacket 60% above its own cost, later advertises it at 25% off the marked price, and at the register takes an extra 10% off the advertised sale price. A customer pays $162.00 for a jacket before tax. What was the store's cost for that jacket?

  • A. $135.00
  • B. $129.60
  • C. $155.77
  • D. $150.00

Answer: D. The register price is cost × 1.60 × 0.75 × 0.90 = cost × 1.08, so the cost is 162.00 ÷ 1.08 = $150.00.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you multiply successive percent changes instead of adding them. Choice C, $155.77, comes from combining the two discounts into a single 35%-off step and dividing by 1.60 × 0.65 — successive discounts compound, they don't just add together.

What changed: the enhanced ACT Math test

Per ACT's own description of the test, the enhanced Math test — now standard for national online testing since a spring 2025 pilot, and for national paper and international testing since September 2025 — runs 45 questions in 50 minutes, down from the legacy format's 60 questions in 60 minutes. Two other changes matter just as much as the shorter length: every question now offers four answer choices instead of five, and a calculator is permitted for the entire section rather than being restricted. The enhanced Math test also drops any reading passages. Of the 45 questions on a given form, 41 are scored; the remaining items are unscored and used for test development. State and district (school-day) administrations were scheduled to move to the enhanced format by spring 2026, with science and writing optionality decided at the contract level for those test-takers. See ACT's own test enhancements page for the full rollout and section-by-section breakdown.

The other sections, and how the Composite score changed

The full enhanced ACT also includes English (50 questions, 35 minutes) and Reading (36 questions, 40 minutes); Science (40 questions, 40 minutes) is now optional rather than required. That optionality changes how your score works: the 1-36 Composite score is now built only from English, Math and Reading. Science no longer factors into the Composite at all. If you take the Science test alongside Math, you receive a separate STEM score instead. Writing remains a separate, optional test on its own scale. None of this changes the familiar 1-36 score range or existing college benchmarks for English, Math and Reading.

What the Math test actually covers

ACT's own math test description splits the section into two reporting categories. Preparing for Higher Math carries about 80% of the test and is further broken into five areas: Number & Quantity (about 10-12%), Algebra (about 17-20%), Functions (about 17-20%), Geometry (about 17-20%), and Statistics & Probability (about 12-15%). Integrating Essential Skills — multi-step word problems that blend percentages, rates, measurement and basic statistics, like Questions 1 and 4 above — carries the remaining 20%. Because the five Preparing for Higher Math areas are reported as ranges rather than fixed percentages, your own score report will show the exact count of items you saw in each category on your specific test form.

A worked example: budgeting study time against the real weights

Say you can commit 25 total hours to ACT Math review before test day. Using the midpoint of each published range as a rough weight instead of splitting time evenly across six topics, the hours should break down roughly like this:

  • Integrating Essential Skills (20%): 25 × 0.20 = 5 hours
  • Algebra (≈18.5%): 25 × 0.185 ≈ 4.5 hours
  • Functions (≈18.5%): 25 × 0.185 ≈ 4.5 hours
  • Geometry (≈18.5%): 25 × 0.185 ≈ 4.5 hours
  • Statistics & Probability (≈13.5%): 25 × 0.135 ≈ 3.5 hours
  • Number & Quantity (≈11%): 25 × 0.11 ≈ 2.5 hours (rounded to keep the total near 25)

That's roughly 5 + 4.5 + 4.5 + 4.5 + 3.5 + 2.5 = 25 hours, weighted toward Algebra, Functions and Geometry — together close to 55% of the test — rather than giving each of the six topics an equal sixth of your time.

Retakes, Score Choice and superscoring

ACT's current retest guidance states there is no limit on how many times you can take the ACT, and that students average two to three attempts to reach their goal score. You choose which test date's scores to send to colleges through Score Choice — colleges only see what you submit, though some schools ask applicants to submit every score from every sitting, so check each school's own policy. If you've tested more than once, ACT also automatically calculates a Superscore from your best English, Math and Reading scores across sittings once you have scores from at least two administrations; no separate request is needed, though individual colleges decide for themselves whether they'll use it. See ACT's own retest guidance and superscore FAQ for details.

What it costs

As of ACT's current fee schedule, the base test — English, Math and Reading — costs $70. Adding the optional Science test costs $5 more, and adding the optional Writing test costs $25 more; taking everything (ACT plus Science plus Writing) totals $100. Retaking the test is billed as a new registration at the same fee structure; there's no separate discounted retake price. Fees are reviewed periodically, so confirm the exact current amount on ACT's own registration page before you pay.
