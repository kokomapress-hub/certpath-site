GED Math Test Day: Format, Calculator, Retake Rules

The GED Mathematical Reasoning test runs 115 minutes in two parts, and the calculator rule is the single detail that trips up the most test-takers: Part 1 is worked entirely by hand, and the calculator — GED Testing Service's own onscreen TI-30XS, or a physical TI-30XS you bring yourself — only becomes available once you reach Part 2. The format: one test, two very different parts

GED Testing Service's own test-subjects page lays out the Mathematical Reasoning test as a single, computer-based session with a 115-minute time limit. That total includes about 2 minutes of instructions and a final review, plus a 3-minute break between the two parts. Most major test-prep providers put the item count at approximately 46 questions, split so that the first handful come before the calculator is available and the remainder come after — confirm the exact current count on GED Testing Service's own site, since item counts on standardized tests are periodically revised.

Content is drawn from four topic areas, per GED Testing Service: Basic Math, Geometry, Basic Algebra, and Graphs and Functions. GED Testing Service does not publish a fixed percentage weight for each area the way some professional-certification exams do, so the safest study approach is even, steady coverage across all four rather than betting heavily on one. Questions aren't limited to standard multiple choice, either — expect drag-and-drop, fill-in-the-blank, select-an-area, and drop-down formats mixed in, which means you'll want to practice with an interface that uses those formats, not just a flat multiple-choice quiz.

The calculator rule, explained

Per GED Testing Service's official test-subjects page, an onscreen calculator becomes available only during Part 2 of the Math test. If you test at a physical Test Center, you're also allowed to bring your own TI-30XS calculator rather than relying on the onscreen version — but that device isn't available at all during Part 1. GED Testing Service also provides a calculator reference sheet and a separate math formula sheet during the test, so you don't need to memorize formulas like the area of a circle or the slope formula; you do need to know which formula to reach for and how to plug numbers into it quickly.

Because Part 1 has to be solved without any calculator, it's worth rehearsing basic arithmetic by hand before test day. Here's the kind of quick mental math Part 1 rewards:

Worked example (no calculator): A store marks a $64 jacket down 25%, then charges 8% sales tax on the sale price. What's the final price?

  • 25% of $64 = $64 ÷ 4 = $16, so the sale price is $64 − $16 = $48.
  • 8% tax on $48: 10% of $48 is $4.80; 1% of $48 is $0.48. So 8% = $4.80 − (2 × $0.48) = $4.80 − $0.96 = $3.84.
  • Final price: $48 + $3.84 = $51.84.

Breaking a percent into 10%-and-1% chunks like that, instead of trying to multiply by 0.08 in your head, is a habit worth building well before Part 1 — the real test won't give you a calculator to fall back on if the arithmetic gets away from you.

How GED Math is scored

GED Testing Service scores every subject test, Math included, on a 100-200 scale:

  • 145-164: Passing. You need at least 145 on each of the four GED subject tests (Math, Reasoning Through Language Arts, Science, and Social Studies) to earn the GED credential — passing Math alone doesn't complete the credential.
  • 165-174: GED College Ready. This band can qualify you for a waiver from some college placement testing or developmental coursework, depending on the receiving institution's own policy.
  • 175-200: GED College Ready + Credit. According to GED Testing Service, a score in this range on a given subject can translate into college credit hours at participating institutions — GED Testing Service's own materials cite examples like 3 credits for Math, 3 for Science, 3 for Social Studies and 1 for Humanities, though exactly how many credits you receive, and whether a given school participates at all, depends entirely on that institution's own program.

Because College Ready and College Ready + Credit are meaningfully higher bars than the 145 pass line, it's worth deciding before you study which target you're actually aiming for — "pass" and "qualify for college credit" call for different amounts of prep time.

If you don't pass: the official retake rules

GED Testing Service publishes its retake policy directly on GED.com, and the rules differ depending on how you test:

  • In-person Test Center: After failing a subject, you can take two more retests with no restriction between them. If you fail a third or later retest, you must wait 60 days before your next attempt.
  • Online Exam: Eligibility and retake rules depend on your state. GED Testing Service directs test-takers to their state's own policy page and "Test Retake Policies" tab rather than publishing one national rule.
  • Cost: GED Testing Service waives its own portion of the fee for retests of a failed subject, though states and individual test centers can set their own prices on top of that. The reduced-price retake window applies to two retests taken within 12 months of your original full-price test; after that, pricing can return to the standard rate.

Some states layer additional requirements on top of GED Testing Service's baseline — extra waiting periods or different fee structures — so check your own state's GED.com policy page before you schedule a retake, rather than assuming the national baseline is the whole picture.

Test-day checklist

  • Confirm your testing format ahead of time. Test Center and Online Exam rules differ, especially around calculators and retakes — know which one you're registered for.
  • Bring valid, acceptable photo ID. Check your state's GED.com policy page for the exact ID requirements before test day; they vary by state.
  • Decide on your calculator in advance. If you're testing at a physical center and want to use your own TI-30XS instead of the onscreen version, confirm your center allows it beforehand.
  • Practice Part 1 without any calculator. Build comfort with percent, fraction and whole-number arithmetic done by hand, since that's exactly what the first part of the test demands.
  • Get familiar with the formula sheet before test day. It's provided during the test, but knowing it ahead of time saves you from hunting through it mid-question.
  • Practice the non-multiple-choice question types. Drag-and-drop, fill-in-the-blank, select-an-area and drop-down formats behave differently than a plain multiple-choice quiz, so rehearse with an interface that uses them.
  • Plan around the full 115 minutes, including the short break between Part 1 and Part 2, so pacing doesn't catch you off guard.
