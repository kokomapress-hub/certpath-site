How Hard Is the CCRN Exam? What AACN's Test Plan Says

"How hard is the CCRN exam" is one of the most common questions ICU nurses type into a search bar before they schedule a test date. The honest answer is: it depends less on general nursing knowledge and more on whether you can apply that knowledge fast, under pressure, to the specific body systems AACN's own test plan says it will ask about. Here's what that test plan actually says — not a guess, not a forum post, but the categories and weights AACN publishes.

The basic format: 150 questions, 3 hours

  • 150 multiple-choice questions total — 125 are scored and 25 are unscored pretest items AACN uses to evaluate future questions. You won't know which 25 those are, so every question deserves your full attention.
  • 3-hour time limit — that works out to about 72 seconds per question on average, though the exam isn't timed section by section, so you can move faster through questions you're sure of and spend more time on the ones that need it.
  • Computer-based delivery at an approved testing center, with results available shortly after you finish.

What the exam is actually testing: the 80/20 split

This is the part most candidates skip past, and it's the part that matters most. AACN's official Adult CCRN test plan splits the 125 scored questions into two categories:

Clinical Judgment — 80% of the exam

This is the bulk of the test, and it's organized by body system rather than by nursing task. The two heaviest categories are Cardiovascular and a combined Endocrine/Hematology/Gastrointestinal/Renal/Integumentary category, which AACN weights as the single largest content area on the exam. The rest of Clinical Judgment covers additional body-system categories, all asking you to apply assessment findings, recognize a deteriorating patient, and choose the right intervention — not just recall a fact.

Professional Caring and Ethical Practice — 20% of the exam

The remaining fifth of the exam covers the non-physiological side of critical care nursing: advocacy, ethical decision-making, communication with patients and families, and end-of-life care. It's built on the AACN Synergy Model for Patient Care, which frames nursing competencies around matching a nurse's characteristics to a patient's needs — a framework worth understanding on its own terms, not just memorizing as exam trivia.

Worked example: turning percentages into a question count

Because AACN publishes the split as percentages of the 125 scored questions, you can estimate how many questions you'll actually see in each category:

  • Clinical Judgment: 125 × 0.80 = about 100 questions
  • Professional Caring and Ethical Practice: 125 × 0.20 = about 25 questions

That's the single most useful planning number in this article. If your study time is split evenly between physiology-heavy content and professional-practice content, you're under-preparing for the 80% of the exam that's actually about clinical judgment across body systems.

Who's eligible to sit the exam

Before you can even schedule the CCRN exam, AACN requires a current RN or APRN license plus clinical hours caring for critically ill adult patients, under one of two paths:

  • 1,750 hours in direct care of critically ill adult patients within the last 2 years, or
  • 2,000 hours in direct care of critically ill adult patients within the last 5 years

If you're early in your ICU career and doing the hour math, it's worth checking AACN's eligibility page directly, since the exact accrual rules and any additional recency requirements are confirmed there rather than on third-party sites.

Fees and the retake policy

The exam fee is $260 for AACN members and $375 for non-members. If you don't pass on your first attempt, AACN offers a discounted retest fee of $185 for members and $290 for non-members. You're allowed up to four attempts within a 12-month period, and there's no mandatory waiting period before you can reapply for a retest — you can schedule again as soon as your new application is approved.

A study checklist based on the actual weighting

Rather than studying every topic equally, use the test plan's own weights to prioritize:

  • Start with cardiovascular and the endocrine/hematology/GI/renal/integumentary cluster. These are the heaviest-weighted categories inside Clinical Judgment, so a weak spot here costs you more than a weak spot in a lighter category.
  • Review every other body-system category at least once before test day — Clinical Judgment spans multiple systems, and skipping one to over-study another leaves a gap the exam is likely to find.
  • Don't skip Professional Caring and Ethical Practice. It's a smaller slice at 20%, but it's a distinct skill — recognizing the ethically correct or most patient-centered action — that clinical experience alone doesn't always sharpen.
  • Practice under real time pressure. At roughly 72 seconds a question across a 3-hour block, the exam rewards candidates who've rehearsed pacing, not just content.
  • Work full-length timed sets, not just isolated questions. Endurance over 150 questions is its own skill separate from knowing the material.

What "hard" actually means for this exam

The CCRN exam isn't hard because the nursing content is obscure — most of it reflects situations experienced ICU nurses see regularly. It's hard because it asks you to apply that knowledge quickly, across a wide range of body systems, in a format where two answer choices often look correct until you check them against the specific scenario details. That's a different skill than bedside competence, and it's the skill that structured practice questions are built to train.
