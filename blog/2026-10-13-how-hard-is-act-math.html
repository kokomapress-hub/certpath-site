How Hard Is ACT Math? Format, Weights and Scoring

ACT shortened and restructured the Math test in its 2025 redesign, and a lot of prep material online still describes the old 60-question version. The new format: 45 questions, 50 minutes

ACT's own test-enhancements page lists the current ACT Math section at 45 questions with a 50-minute time limit — about 67 seconds per question. That's a real change from the legacy test, which had 60 questions in 60 minutes. ACT's own counselor blog, The Enhanced ACT Explained, confirms the section is shorter and adds that Math answer choices were trimmed from five options to four. The rest of the test shortened too: per the same enhancements page, English runs 50 questions in 35 minutes, Reading runs 36 questions in 40 minutes, and Science — now optional — runs 40 questions in 40 minutes. Writing, also optional, is a single 40-minute prompt.

ACT's rollout has been staged. The redesigned format became available for national test takers starting in 2025, according to ACT's own counselor blog, with 2025-2026 registrants able to choose their test format and whether to add Science. State, district and international testing moves to the redesigned format starting in spring 2026. If you're testing through your school rather than on a national Saturday date, confirm with your counselor which format you'll actually see — the two can still differ depending on your testing route and date.

What's actually being tested: two reporting categories, not a flat topic list

ACT's official Mathematics Test Description splits every Math question into one of two reporting categories, and each of those breaks down further:

  • Preparing for Higher Math — 80% of the test. This umbrella category covers five subcategories: Number & Quantity (10-12%), Algebra (17-20%), Functions (17-20%), Geometry (17-20%), and Statistics & Probability (12-15%). Together these make up the large majority of the section.
  • Integrating Essential Skills — 20% of the test. Multi-step, nonroutine problems that layer rates, percentages, proportional relationships, area, surface area, volume, averages and medians, and different ways of expressing the same number — often combining two or three of those ideas in a single question rather than testing them in isolation.

ACT also describes a separate "Modeling" thread that runs through both categories rather than standing on its own: questions that ask you to produce, interpret, evaluate or improve a mathematical model. Each modeling question is still counted inside whichever reporting category it belongs to, so it isn't an extra slice of the test — it's a style of question that shows up inside the categories above.

A worked example in the Integrating Essential Skills style

Because a fifth of the test is exactly this kind of layered, multi-step problem, it's worth rehearsing the pattern directly:

Problem: A school orders 18 boxes of markers at $14.50 per box. The supplier takes 12% off the full order, then charges 6% sales tax on the discounted amount. What is the final cost, to the nearest cent?

  • Full order before any discount: 18 × $14.50 = $261.00.
  • After the 12% discount, the school pays 88% of that: $261.00 × 0.88 = $229.68.
  • Tax is 6% of the discounted amount, not the original price: $229.68 × 1.06 = $243.4608, which rounds to $243.46.

The question only becomes hard if you apply the tax to the original $261.00 instead of the discounted $229.68, or if you subtract the discount and tax as flat percentages instead of multiplying them in sequence. Both are exactly the kind of order-of-operations trap Integrating Essential Skills questions are built to catch.

Scoring: the Composite, Science, and the Superscore

Per ACT's own scores page, the Composite score is the average of your English, Math and Reading scores, rounded to the nearest whole number — each section is scored on the familiar 1-36 scale, and so is the Composite. Science and Writing are optional and sit outside the Composite entirely: if you take Science, you get a separate score for it plus a STEM score (an average of your Math and Science scores), but neither one changes your Composite. Writing is scored separately on a 2-12 scale and likewise doesn't touch the Composite.

If you test more than once, ACT's Superscore averages your best section scores across all your test dates, using English, Math and Reading. Many colleges accept Superscores, which means a strong Math score from one sitting can carry forward even if a later sitting's Reading score dips — but policies vary by school, so confirm how a given college actually uses ACT scores before counting on that.

What it costs, and the rules around retesting

Per ACT's own fee page, the base test — English, Math and Reading, no add-ons — costs $70. Adding Science brings the total to $75. Adding Writing instead brings it to $95, and adding both Science and Writing brings it to $100. Registering or changing your test date during the late registration window adds a $42 late fee, and a separate $49 change fee covers switching your test form, test day, or test center.

ACT's own registration page states that you cannot receive scores from more than one test date within the same national test administration — if you test a second time during that window, only the first test's scores are reported. If you want to retest on a different date, ACT's page is direct about the cost: "You must register again and pay the full fee for your test option if you want to retest." There's no discounted retake fee described on that page.

A 4-week prep checklist built around the real weights

  • Week 1 — Algebra, Functions and Geometry. These three subcategories alone make up roughly 51-60% of the test, so start here rather than with whatever topic feels most familiar.
  • Week 2 — Number & Quantity and Statistics & Probability. Smaller slices of the test (10-12% and 12-15%), but still worth dedicated time since together they're close to a quarter of the questions.
  • Week 3 — Integrating Essential Skills. Drill multi-step, layered word problems specifically — discounts-then-tax, unit conversions inside a rate, and other sequences where doing the steps in the wrong order gives you a wrong-but-plausible answer.
  • Week 4 — Timed, full-length practice. At 50 minutes for 45 questions, pacing is unforgiving. Practice under the real clock, not an untimed worksheet, so test day's rhythm is already familiar.
