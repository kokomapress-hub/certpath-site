Free CAPM Practice Test: 4 Exam-Style Questions Explained

The CAPM exam doesn't stop at "define a work breakdown structure" or "name the phases of a project." It hands you two or three sentences of situation, four answers that all sound defensible, and expects you to pick the one PMI's own frameworks actually support. Here are four exam-style practice questions, worked step by step across four different content domains, so you can see exactly what that looks like before test day.

What's actually on the CAPM exam right now

The CAPM is administered by the Project Management Institute (PMI). CertPath Publishing is an independent publisher and is not affiliated with or endorsed by PMI.

Per PMI's CAPM Examination Content Outline and its CAPM certification page, the exam is 150 questions total — 135 scored plus 15 unscored pretest questions mixed in and indistinguishable from the rest — delivered in 180 minutes, split into two 75-question sections with one optional 10-minute break at the halfway point. The four content domains and their current weights are:

  • Project Management Fundamentals and Core Concepts — 36% (the largest domain: basic vocabulary, project vs. programme vs. portfolio, estimating techniques, roles and authority)
  • Business Analysis Frameworks — 27% (requirements gathering and traceability, stakeholder engagement, backlog and roadmap communication)
  • Agile Frameworks and Methodologies — 20% (Scrum events, the sprint goal vs. sprint backlog, definition of done)
  • Predictive, Plan-Based Methodologies — 17% (network diagrams, three-point estimating, earned value, reserves)

That spread is why the CAPM surprises people expecting a "junior PMP": nearly half the exam sits in business analysis and agile territory, not predictive project management.

Question 1: Fundamentals — programme, portfolio or project?

An insurer has eight initiatives, each already chartered as its own project with a separate sponsor, budget and project manager. Five of them each replace part of the ageing claims platform: they share one integration layer and one migration team, none delivers much alone, and the projected savings appear only once all five are in service. The other three are unrelated to the platform and compete with the five for the same investment pool. Management wants the claims savings managed deliberately by whoever can be held to them. How should the five be organised?

  • A. As a portfolio with the other three, because all eight compete for one investment pool and must be balanced against strategy
  • B. As a programme, because coordinating the related initiatives produces benefits none of them can deliver separately
  • C. As a single project, by revoking the five charters and rechartering the work under one project manager
  • D. As five projects with a shared steering committee, because each has its own deliverable, schedule and project manager

Correct answer: B.

Explanation: A programme is a group of related projects managed together to obtain benefits unavailable from managing them individually, which is exactly the shared integration layer and the savings that appear only collectively. The portfolio answer is the strongest trap: the eight initiatives genuinely do compete for one pool, and a portfolio is the right structure for that investment decision, but it does not make the five deliver a joint benefit. Rechartering the five as one project would unnecessarily collapse five already distinct projects; because their coordinated management is needed to realise benefits that none can deliver independently, programme management is the structure that fits the situation.

Skill tested: telling programme management apart from portfolio management when both descriptions technically apply to the same set of initiatives.

The trap: the portfolio answer isn't wrong about the facts — the eight really do compete for one investment pool — it's wrong about what the question is asking, which is how to capture a shared benefit, not how to allocate investment.

Question 2: Predictive — a PERT calculation, worked in full

Two activities run in series on the critical path and both carry three-point estimates. Switchgear installation is 14 days if all pre-work is complete, 20 days under normal conditions and 38 days if the panels arrive damaged. Commissioning is 8 days at best, 12 days most likely and 22 days if the site power supply proves unstable. The estimating standard requires the beta (PERT) distribution. What total duration should be scheduled for the pair?

  • A. 32 days
  • B. 41 days
  • C. 38 days
  • D. 35 days

Correct answer: D.

Explanation: Each activity is weighted as (optimistic + 4 × most likely + pessimistic) ÷ 6: installation gives (14 + 80 + 38) ÷ 6 = 22 days and commissioning gives (8 + 48 + 22) ÷ 6 = 13 days, so the pair totals 35 days. The 38-day answer is the strongest trap: it uses the triangular formula, a plain average of the three values, which under-weights the most likely case. The 32-day answer sums the most likely figures alone, and 41 averages the optimistic and pessimistic totals.

Skill tested: applying the PERT weighted-average formula correctly instead of defaulting to a simple average of the three estimates.

The trap: the triangular average (38 days) is close enough to the correct answer, and easy enough to compute in your head, that it's the one most people fall for when they're moving quickly.

Question 3: Agile — renegotiating scope mid-sprint

Six days into a two-week sprint, testing of a partly built reporting story shows that two of its five acceptance criteria will take far longer than expected. The product owner says the sprint goal still depends on the story, but the two criteria are not what the goal needs. The developers ask how the sprint scope may be changed. What is correct under Scrum?

  • A. The sprint must be cancelled and replanned, because the sprint backlog no longer reflects what the developers can deliver
  • B. The product owner may change the story's scope at any time during the sprint, since backlog content is the product owner's decision
  • C. The developers and the product owner may renegotiate the story's scope, provided the sprint goal is not put at risk
  • D. The sprint scope is fixed once the sprint begins, so the story is carried over to the next sprint with its criteria intact

Correct answer: C.

Explanation: The sprint goal is fixed; the scope inside it is clarified and renegotiated between the developers and the product owner as more is learned. Treating the sprint backlog as frozen is the most tempting error, because it sounds like commitment discipline, yet it forces the team to carry over work the goal does not require. The product owner does not impose changes mid-sprint unilaterally, and cancelling a sprint is reserved for a goal that has become obsolete.

Skill tested: knowing exactly what Scrum treats as fixed during a sprint (the goal) versus what stays negotiable (the scope inside it).

The trap: "the sprint is locked once it starts" feels like the disciplined, textbook answer, which is exactly why it's the one CAPM questions use to catch people who've memorized the shape of Scrum without the reasoning behind it.

Question 4: Business Analysis — an orphan requirement before build

During a pre-build review, the business analyst finds that one approved requirement, a configurable dashboard layout, traces forward to design and test cases but traces back to no business objective, while every other requirement traces to one. The sponsor is not aware of the requirement, and building it would consume about three weeks. What should the analyst do?

  • A. Remove the requirement from the baseline, since a requirement that traces to no business objective delivers no value
  • B. Add a business objective that the dashboard supports, so the traceability matrix is complete before the build begins
  • C. Take the gap to the sponsor and the product owner to confirm whether the requirement should be kept, changed or dropped
  • D. Keep the requirement and build it, since it is already approved, designed and covered by the prepared test cases

Correct answer: C.

Explanation: Traceability is a detection tool, not a decision tool; an orphan requirement is a finding to be resolved by the people who own scope and value. Removing it is the strongest trap, because the analyst has spotted a genuine candidate for scope creep, yet unilateral removal exceeds the analyst's authority and the objective may simply be missing from the record. Writing an objective to fit the requirement reverses the logic, and building it spends three weeks unexamined.

Skill tested: recognizing the limit of a business analyst's own authority — finding a gap is the job, deciding unilaterally what to do about it usually isn't.

The trap: removing the requirement feels like the responsible, decisive move, but the CAPM consistently tests whether you'll escalate a scope decision to the people who actually own it instead of making the call yourself.

Who these questions are really testing

Notice what each wrong answer does: it applies a real, correct idea to the wrong situation. A portfolio really does balance competing investments — just not this problem. The triangular average really is a valid shortcut — just not the one the standard called for here. A frozen sprint backlog sounds disciplined — but it isn't what Scrum says. Unilateral removal would tidy the baseline — but it isn't the analyst's call. CAPM question writers rarely test a memorized definition; they test whether you can match the right idea to the facts in front of you.

These four also span all four content domains on purpose: candidates often over-prepare on Predictive and Fundamentals material, since it maps to a traditional "project management" mental model, and under-prepare on Agile and Business Analysis, which together make up 47% of the exam.

Eligibility and cost, in brief

To sit the CAPM exam, PMI requires a secondary degree — a high school diploma, GED or global equivalent — plus at least 23 hours of project management education completed before you apply. PMI removed the older 1,500-hour project-experience alternative in August 2024, so education is now the only prerequisite beyond the degree, which is part of why CAPM has become a common entry point for students and career changers with no formal PM experience.

PMI's published fee is $225 for members and $300 for non-members. If you don't pass on your first attempt, you may retake the exam up to three times within your one-year eligibility window, with a reexamination fee on the second and third attempts; failing all three means a required one-year wait before you can reapply. Fees and policy details can change, so confirm current numbers on PMI's own CAPM certification page before you register.
