4 GED Math questions, worked step by step

These four come from CertPath's free 25-question GED Math sample set and are quoted exactly as written there.

Question 1 — Rational numbers: stacking a discount, a tax and a rebate

A phone is listed at $540.00. A store takes 15% off the listed price, then adds 6% sales tax on the discounted price, and the manufacturer later sends the buyer a $25.00 rebate cheque. What is the buyer's net cost for the phone?

  • A. $466.40
  • B. $461.54
  • C. $460.04
  • D. $486.54

Answer: B. The discounted price is 540.00 × 0.85 = $459.00. Tax makes it 459.00 × 1.06 = $486.54, and the rebate brings the net cost to 486.54 − 25.00 = $461.54.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you apply each step in the order the stem gives it — discount, then tax, then rebate — instead of collapsing them into one shortcut. Choice A, $466.40, comes from combining the percents into a single 1 − 0.15 + 0.06 = 0.91 factor; that skips the fact that tax is charged on the already-discounted price, not on the original listed price.

Question 2 — Measurement: a sliding ladder and the Pythagorean theorem

A 26-foot ladder leans against a vertical wall with its base 10 feet from the wall. A worker then slides the base 4 feet farther from the wall, and the ladder keeps resting against the wall. To the nearest tenth of a foot, how far down the wall does the top of the ladder move?

  • A. 12.0 feet
  • B. 4.0 feet
  • C. 21.9 feet
  • D. 2.1 feet

Answer: D. At first the top is √(26² − 10²) = √576 = 24 feet up. With the base 14 feet out, the top is √(26² − 14²) = √480 ≈ 21.9 feet up, so it drops about 24 − 21.9 = 2.1 feet.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you recognize the ladder, wall and ground as a right triangle whose hypotenuse never changes, then solve the Pythagorean theorem twice — once for each base position. Choice B, 4.0 feet, assumes the top slides down by exactly as much as the base slides out; because the relationship is not linear, the top actually moves much less than the base does.

Question 3 — Probability: drawing two socks without replacement

A drawer holds 5 black socks, 4 navy socks, and 3 grey socks, all loose. Two socks are pulled out at random without looking and without putting the first one back. To the nearest hundredth, what is the probability that the two socks are the same colour?

  • A. 0.15
  • B. 0.35
  • C. 0.29
  • D. 0.71

Answer: C. There are C(12,2) = 66 equally likely pairs. Same-colour pairs number C(5,2) + C(4,2) + C(3,2) = 10 + 6 + 3 = 19, so the probability is 19/66 ≈ 0.29.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you understand that pulling socks "without putting the first one back" changes the total available for the second draw. Choice B, 0.35, treats the two draws as independent, as though the first sock went back in the drawer: (5/12)² + (4/12)² + (3/12)² ≈ 0.35. Without replacement, the second draw only has 11 socks left, not 12.

Question 4 — Graphs and functions: the equation of a perpendicular line

In the coordinate plane, one line has the equation 2x + 3y = 12. A second line passes through the point (6, −2) and is perpendicular to the first line. What is the equation of the second line?

  • A. y = (3/2)x − 11
  • B. y = (2/3)x − 6
  • C. y = −(3/2)x + 7
  • D. y = −(2/3)x + 2

Answer: A. Writing the given line as y = −(2/3)x + 4 shows its slope is −2/3, so a perpendicular line has slope 3/2, the opposite reciprocal. Through (6, −2): −2 = (3/2)(6) + b gives b = −11, so y = (3/2)x − 11.

Skill tested and common trap: this checks whether you know a perpendicular slope is the negative reciprocal of the original, not just its opposite sign. Choice D, with slope −2/3, is parallel to the given line rather than perpendicular to it — flipping only the sign and not the fraction is the single most common mistake on this question type.

What's actually on the GED Math test

Per GED Testing Service's own Test Subjects page, the Mathematical Reasoning test has 46 questions and a 115-minute time limit, with no separate timed sections inside that window. Question formats include multiple choice, fill-in-the-blank, drop-down, hot spot and drag-and-drop — not just the four-option format used in the sample above. The calculator rule is specific and trips people up: the first 5 questions must be answered with no calculator at all, and only starting at question 6 does an onscreen TI-30XS MultiView calculator become available for the remaining 41 questions. If you're testing at a physical test center, you can also bring your own handheld TI-30XS MultiView rather than relying on the onscreen version.

What's tested, and how the test splits between the two major areas

The test covers two major content areas, each broken into two reporting categories:

  • Quantitative Problem Solving — about 45% of the test.
    • Rational numbers — about 25%. Percents, ratios, signed numbers and multi-step money problems, like the discount-tax-rebate stack in Question 1.
    • Measurement — about 20%. Geometry, area, volume and the Pythagorean theorem, like the sliding ladder in Question 2.
  • Algebraic Problem Solving — about 55% of the test.
    • Expressions and equations — about 30%. Linear and quadratic equations, systems of equations, and expanding polynomial expressions.
    • Graphs and functions — about 25%. Slope, linear and quadratic functions, and coordinate geometry, like the perpendicular-line question in Question 4.

Probability and data-analysis items, like the sock problem in Question 3, are folded into the Quantitative Problem Solving area rather than scored as a separate category. Because Algebraic Problem Solving alone makes up more than half the test, a study plan that spends equal time on every topic ends up under-preparing for the single biggest share of questions.

A worked example: budgeting study time against the real split

Say you can commit 20 total hours to GED Math review before test day. Weighted to the published reporting-category percentages instead of split evenly across four topics, that time should break down like this:

  • Rational numbers (25%): 20 × 0.25 = 5 hours
  • Measurement (20%): 20 × 0.20 = 4 hours
  • Expressions and equations (30%): 20 × 0.30 = 6 hours
  • Graphs and functions (25%): 20 × 0.25 = 5 hours

That's 5 + 4 + 6 + 5 = 20 hours, matching the test's own weighting rather than giving each of the four categories an equal 5-hour quarter. At 4 hours a week, that same split stretches across 5 weeks, with expressions and equations getting the extra attention its 30% share earns.

Scoring and what counts as passing

Each of the four GED subject tests, including Mathematical Reasoning, is scored on a 100-200 scale. A score of 145 or higher is passing. Scoring above that minimum earns additional recognition: 165-174 is GED College Ready, and 175 or above is GED College Ready + Credit, which some colleges accept toward course credit. These score bands are published on GED Testing Service's own Test Subjects page alongside the format details above.

Cost and where to register

GED test fees are set state by state rather than nationally, so the price per subject (and any discounts or vouchers available through local adult-education programs) varies depending on where you test. GED Testing Service's own site lets you select your state to see the exact current fee before you register — confirm it there rather than relying on a number quoted elsewhere, since state pricing changes periodically.
